Logarithmorum canonis descriptio, for floating point

mirifici

A log-space number type for Rust. Log<F> stores a non-negative number as its natural logarithm, so multiplication becomes addition and 10−5000 stays a number.

I. The wonderful canon

Seeing there is nothing (right well-beloved Students of the Mathematics) that is so troublesome to mathematical practice, nor that doth more molest and hinder calculators, than the multiplications, divisions, square and cubical extractions of great numbers…

John Napier, preface to the Descriptio, in Edward Wright’s translation (1616)

Napier’s remedy was a table that paired every number with a logarithm, so that a product could be read off as a sum. Floating-point numbers have the same trouble in a different form: an f64 cannot go below about 10−308, so a long product of probabilities quietly becomes zero. The cure is the same. Store the logarithm, and add.

Why the natural logarithm? Grégoire de Saint-Vincent showed in 1647 that the area under the hyperbola y = 1/t over [a, b] equals the area over [ka, kb]; his student de Sarasa saw that this makes the area a logarithm. Drag the point: the two shaded regions always match, because stretching an interval by a factor adds a fixed amount of area.

II. Where floats fall to zero

Multiply n independent probabilities of size p, as a hidden Markov model or a likelihood does. Plain f64 runs into its floor and returns zero; Log keeps the value, prints it, and can still compare and add it.

III. Arithmetic on the logarithms

Operators act on values; the work happens on the stored logarithms ℓa = ln a. Every edge case is defined, and none of them produces NaN: indeterminate forms such as 0·∞ return None from the checked_* methods.

Why log1mexp switches formulas at ln 2

Computing ln(1 − e^−d) directly fails for small d, where e^−d rounds to 1 and the result to −∞. Following Mächler, the crate uses

Each branch is evaluated where its inner function is accurate. The same care runs through the crate: ln_1p for additions, Neumaier summation for long products, and double-double constants for base 10 and base 2.

Mercator’s 1668 series ln(1+t) = t − t²/2 + … is why ln_1p exists at all: for t = 10⁻²⁰, forming 1 + t first loses t entirely.

IV. Accuracy, measured

Absolute error in the stored log is relative error in the value. Each operation is checked against 60-digit references computed with mpmath, in units of ε·max(1, |ln x|). A correctly rounded result scores at most 0.5. The bars below show the worst case over thousands of inputs; the tick is the documented bound that CI enforces on Linux, macOS and Windows.

V. A short history, where it lives in the API

  1. 1614

    John Napier, Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio

    Twenty years of computation turned the products of spherical trigonometry into sums of table entries. He named the numbers logarithms, from logos (ratio) and arithmos (number).

    In the API: Log itself, and * as + on the stored logs.

  2. 1620

    Jost Bürgi, Arithmetische und Geometrische Progress Tabulen

    Independently of Napier, the Swiss clockmaker set red numbers in arithmetic progression beside black numbers in geometric progression, 10⁸·1.0001ⁿ.

    In the API: powi, one step along such a table.

  3. 1624

    Henry Briggs, Arithmetica Logarithmica

    Base ten, with log 1 = 0 and log 10 = 1: the logarithms of 1–20,000 and 90,000–100,000 to fourteen places, by hand. Fourteen places is about what an f64 holds for a five-digit number.

    In the API: log10, and the decimal exponent of Display.

  4. 1647 · 1649

    Grégoire de Saint-Vincent & Alphonse Antonio de Sarasa

    Areas under the hyperbola over geometrically spaced intervals are equal, so the area behaves as a logarithm: ln x = ∫ dt/t.

    In the API: ln, the one logarithm the type stores.

  5. 1668

    Nicholas Mercator, Logarithmotechnia

    The series for ln(1+t), and, by most accounts, the first use of the phrase natural logarithm.

    In the API: the ln_1p inside every addition.

VI. Leading digits, beyond the float range

A number has leading digit d exactly when the fractional part of its base-10 logarithm lies in [log d, log(d+1)). Data spread evenly over many orders of magnitude has its logarithms spread evenly modulo one, so digit d appears with frequency

This is Benford’s law. The chart counts the leading digits of 2¹ … 2¹⁰⁰⁰⁰ and of 1! … 3000!, computed here in log space, as the crate’s benford example does. Almost all of them overflow an f64: 2¹⁰²⁴ and 171! already do.

VII. Use

# Cargo.toml
[dependencies]
mirifici = "0.1"
# no_std, or bit-identical results on every platform:
# mirifici = { version = "0.1", features = ["libm"] }
use mirifici::Log;

// The likelihood of 100,000 observations.
let likelihood: Log = data.iter()
    .map(|&x| density(x))
    .product();

let tiny: Log = "2.5e-90210".parse()?;
assert_eq!(tiny.to_string(), "2.5e-90210");
assert!(tiny < likelihood);

Guarantees

Domain
x ≥ 0, stored as ln x in one f32 or f64; SignedLog for signed values.
No NaN
Invalid inputs are errors; indeterminate forms are None or a panic, never NaN.
Range
e^(±1.8·10³⁰⁸), printed and parsed exactly in scientific notation.
Accuracy
Documented bounds for every operation, tested against 60-digit references.
Stability
API, accuracy bounds and the serialized form are covered by a written stability policy.
Portable
#![no_std], no allocation, MSRV 1.85; serde, num-traits, approx and bytemuck behind features.
